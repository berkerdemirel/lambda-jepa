Paper overview

Emergence of Invariance and Disentanglement in Deep Representations

Alessandro Achille and Stefano Soatto. Journal of Machine Learning Research 18, 2018. The paper develops an information-theoretic story for why deep networks trained with noise, bottlenecks, SGD-like implicit regularization, or an explicit information regularizer can learn representations that are sufficient for the task while becoming insensitive to nuisance variation and less statistically entangled.

JMLR 18 (2018), pages 1-34 Submitted 01/17; revised 04/18; published 06/18 arXiv:1706.01350v3 Keywords: representation learning, PAC-Bayes, information bottleneck, flat minima

One-paragraph summary

The central claim is a chain of implications. First, if a representation \(z\) is sufficient for predicting \(y\), then minimizing its information about the input \(I(z;x)\) makes it maximally insensitive to nuisance variables \(n\) that are irrelevant to \(y\) Sec. 3, Prop. 3.1. Second, deep architectures with noisy or bottlenecked layers naturally reduce information through the Markov chain of layers Sec. 3, Cor. 3.4; Prop. 3.5. Third, the paper shifts the information bottleneck from activations to weights: regularizing the information that learned weights w contain about the dataset D, \(I(w;D)\), controls memorization and yields a practical loss related to variational dropout and PAC-Bayes Secs. 4, 6. Finally, under stochastic multiplicative-weight assumptions, \(I(w;D)\) bounds the information and total correlation of layer representations, connecting flat minima, invariance, disentanglement, and generalization Sec. 5.

Key takeaways

1

Minimal means information-minimal, not low-dimensional. The paper minimizes \(I(z;x)\), not \(\dim z\), and argues this is what matters for invariance Sec. 3.

2

The weight information bottleneck is the paper's main methodological twist. It regularizes \(H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta I(w;D)\), rather than only studying \(I(z;x)\) post hoc Eq. (3).

3

Random labels expose memorization cost. For random labels, the theory predicts a transition near \(\beta=1\); experiments show sharp overfitting-to-underfitting transitions across architectures Sec. 7.1, Figs. 1-2.

4

The strongest theoretical links need stochastic-weight assumptions. The duality bounds rely on multiplicative noise and distributional approximations, not arbitrary deterministic networks Sec. 5, Props. 5.1-5.2.

Problem and motivation

The paper tries to reconcile two observations about deep learning.

The representation-learning observation

Deep networks often learn internal activations that are insensitive to nuisance variation such as translation, deformation, occlusion, background, or other task-irrelevant factors. Some invariance is architectural, for example convolution and pooling, but the paper emphasizes complex, dataset-specific, non-group nuisances that architecture alone does not encode Sec. 1.1.

The optimization/generalization observation

Highly overparameterized networks can fit random labels, yet with real labels they often generalize. The paper argues this is not inconsistent with bias-variance ideas if complexity is measured by information in the weights rather than by parameter count Secs. 1, 4, 7.2.

The paper's question: If the standard loss does not explicitly ask for invariance or disentanglement, why can these properties emerge under ordinary deep-network training?

Main contributions

1. Minimal sufficient representations are invariant

Claim: among sufficient representations, minimizing \(I(z;x)\) minimizes information about nuisances \(n\). In deterministic-label cases, the residual term in the equivalence vanishes Sec. 3, Prop. 3.1.

Why it matters: invariance becomes an information-compression objective rather than a separately engineered property.

2. Bottlenecks and stacking promote invariance

Claim: if layers form a Markov chain and the later layer remains sufficient, information bottlenecks such as dimension reduction or noise make later representations more insensitive to nuisances Sec. 3, Cor. 3.4; Prop. 3.5.

Support: this follows from Data Processing plus Proposition 3.1, not from a new training algorithm.

3. Cross-entropy contains an overfitting term

Claim: the training cross-entropy can be decomposed into intrinsic error, sufficiency, efficiency, and a negative term \(-I(y;w|x,\theta)\) that rewards memorizing labels not informative of the true data-generating parameter Sec. 4, Eq. (2); App. C.3.

Why it matters: it motivates bounding this term by weight information or by a PAC-Bayes KL term.

4. Information bottleneck for weights

Claim: minimizing \(H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta I(w;D)\) controls memorization. With tractable approximations it becomes a KL-regularized stochastic-weight objective Sec. 4, Eqs. (3)-(5).

Why it matters: it turns information in the weights into a model-complexity measure and a trainable regularizer.

5. Duality between weights and activations

Claim: under multiplicative stochastic weights and Gaussian approximations, \(I(z;x)+TC(z)\) is controlled by the information in the weights. Thus reducing \(I(w;D)\) pushes activations toward minimality and lower total correlation Sec. 5, Props. 5.1-5.2; Cor. 5.3.

6. Empirical validation

Claim tested: regularizing information in weights predicts phase transitions on random labels, recovers a bias-variance-like curve in \(\beta\), and reduces measured nuisance information in cluttered MNIST representations Sec. 7, Figs. 1-4.

Representation concepts

Sufficient: \(z\) keeps all information about the task available in \(x\): \(I(z;y)=I(x;y)\).

Minimal: among sufficient representations, \(z\) minimizes \(I(z;x)\).

Invariant to \(n\): \(I(z;n)=0\). If zero is impossible, the paper uses maximally insensitive for minimizing \(I(z;n)\) among sufficient representations Sec. 2.2.

The two bottlenecks

Activation IB: \(H(y|z)+\beta I(z;x)\), the classical representation bottleneck Eq. (1).

Weight IB: \(H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta I(w;D)\), the paper's training objective for stochastic weights Eq. (3).

Mathematical setup and assumptions

Data and task model

The training samples are drawn from an unknown distribution \(p_\theta(x,y)\). In Sec. 4, \(\theta\) is treated as a random variable under an unknown prior, but the paper notes this Bayesian framing is not necessary for the PAC-Bayes route in Sec. 6.

A nuisance is defined by marginal independence from the task, \(I(y;n)=0\), not by conditional independence \(I(y;n|x)=0\). Appendix D explains that the conditional definition would classify too much as nuisance when \(y\) is deterministic given \(x\).

Representation model

Representations are stochastic functions of inputs: \(p(z|x)\) defines \(z\), and the chain \(y\to x\to z\) holds. Layer-wise results use Markov chains \(x\to z_1\to z_2\to\cdots\to z_L\). The paper notes ResNets do not literally satisfy this for every layer because of skip connections, but their blocks can still be treated this way Sec. 3.

Main theoretical results

The cards below select the results that carry the paper's argument. Routine appendix proofs and minor corollaries are summarized only when they clarify the main chain.

CoreRepresentationSec. 3, Prop. 3.1

Result 1: Minimal sufficient representations are maximally invariant

If \(z\) already preserves all task-relevant information about \(y\), then any remaining information it has about \(x\) is, in the paper's sense, nuisance information. Therefore, compressing \(z\) by reducing \(I(z;x)\) while maintaining sufficiency also reduces \(I(z;n)\).

For deterministic labels \(y=f(x)\), the residual in the theorem vanishes, so the connection is especially clean.

\[ I(z;n) \le I(z;x)-I(x;y). \]

For a particular nuisance construction, equality holds up to a residual:

\[ I(z;n)=I(z;x)-I(x;y)-\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon=I(z;y|n)-I(x;y),\qquad 0\le \epsilon\le H(y|x). \]

Since sufficiency means \(I(z;y)=I(x;y)\), \(I(x;y)\) is the irreducible task information. The excess \(I(z;x)-I(x;y)\) is what can carry nuisance variation.

  1. Use the Markov chain \((y,n)\to x\to z\), so by Data Processing \(I(z;y,n)\le I(z;x)\).
  2. Expand \(I(z;y,n)=I(z;n)+I(z;y|n)\).
  3. Because \(y\) and \(n\) are independent, \(I(z;y|n)\ge I(z;y)\).
  4. Use sufficiency \(I(z;y)=I(x;y)\) to get the bound.

This is not saying any compressed representation is good. If compression destroys sufficiency, it can discard task information. It also does not prove invariance to a named real-world transformation unless that transformation fits the nuisance definition for the task.

ArchitectureNoise / bottlenecksSec. 3

Result 2: Bottlenecks and stacking can increase invariance

A layer that receives less information from the previous layer, due to dimension reduction, pooling, dropout, or injected noise, can be more invariant if it remains sufficient. Stacking layers tends to remove information by Data Processing; if the final layer still solves the task, it is more nuisance-insensitive than earlier layers.

For \(x\to z_1\to z_2\) with a bottleneck \(I(z_1;z_2)<I(z_1;x)\), if \(z_2\) is sufficient then

\[ I(z_2;n)\le I(z_1;z_2)-I(x;y). \]

For a layer chain \(x\to z_1\to\cdots\to z_L\), if \(z_L\) is sufficient, then \(z_L\) is more insensitive to nuisances than preceding layers Prop. 3.5.

Apply Proposition 3.1 to later layers and combine it with Data Processing: later layers cannot contain more information about earlier layers than what passed through their channel.

The paper explicitly warns against reading this as "more layers always help." The conclusion assumes the last layer is sufficient. In practice, deeper networks can be harder to optimize, and architectures with skip connections need block-level rather than layer-level Markov reasoning.

OptimizationSec. 4, Eq. (2)App. C.3

Result 3: Cross-entropy has a negative memorization term

The training loss can be reduced either by learning task structure or by memorizing training labels. The decomposition isolates memorization as a negative term, so unregularized cross-entropy may reward it.

\[ H_{p,q}(y|x,w)= \underbrace{H(y|x,\theta)}_{\text{intrinsic error}}+ \underbrace{I(\theta;y|x,w)}_{\text{sufficiency}}+ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x,w} KL(p(y|x,w)\|q(y|x,w))}_{\text{efficiency}}- \underbrace{I(y;w|x,\theta)}_{\text{overfitting}}. \]

The main text labels this Eq. (2); Appendix C.3 restates it as Eq. (8).

The proof rewrites cross-entropy as entropy plus a KL mismatch term, then uses conditional mutual-information identities to decompose \(H_p(y|x,w)\). The negative sign appears because knowing weights can reveal label information beyond what \(x\) and \(\theta\) explain.

The decomposition is explanatory; it does not make \(I(y;w|x,\theta)\) easy to compute. The paper therefore bounds or regularizes it indirectly.

Training objectiveSecs. 4.1-4.2PAC-Bayes in Sec. 6

Result 4: Regularize information in the weights

To fit real structure, weights need information about the data-generating parameter \(\theta\), which can be bounded independently of sample size in the paper's model. To memorize random labels, they need information that grows with the number of samples. Penalizing \(I(w;D)\) therefore targets memorization.

\[ L(q(w|D))=H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta I(w;D). \tag{3} \]

Since \(I(w;D)=\mathbb{E}_D KL(q(w|D)\|q(w))\) is generally intractable, use

\[ L(q(w|D))=H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta KL(q(w|D)\|p(w)), \tag{4} \]

and, with a factorized approximation \(\tilde q(w)=\prod_i q(w_i)\),

\[ L(q(w|D))=H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\beta KL(q(w|D)\|\tilde q(w)). \tag{5} \]

The argument bounds the overfitting term in the cross-entropy decomposition by information stored in the weights. In Sec. 6, the same practical form also arises from a PAC-Bayes test-error bound, so the paper offers two routes to a KL-regularized weight objective.

The ideal marginal \(q(w)\) is unavailable because it averages over datasets and stochastic training outcomes. The factorized prior is a tractable approximation. With the improper log-uniform prior used for analytic convenience, the PAC-Bayes numerical bound is vacuous, although the optimization objective remains meaningful in the paper's experiments.

Computable complexitySec. 4.2, Prop. 4.1

Result 5: With multiplicative noise, weight information has a simple bound

Under the paper's modeling assumptions, weights are parameterized as

\[ w_i|D\sim \epsilon_i\hat w_i,\qquad \epsilon_i\sim \log\mathcal{N}(-\alpha_i/2,\alpha_i). \]

With a log-uniform factorized prior, the information upper bound becomes

\[ I(w;D)\le \tilde I(w;D)=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(w)}\log \alpha_i + C. \tag{Prop. 4.1} \]

Interpretation: larger weight noise corresponds to less storable information. The constant \(C\) is arbitrary because the prior is improper; the paper drops it for optimization because additive constants do not affect gradients Remark 4.2.

Flat minimaSec. 4.3, Prop. 4.3

Result 6: Flat minima have low information

Plain English: if the loss landscape is flat near a local minimum, weights can be stored with lower precision/noisier perturbations without increasing the loss much. That means the weights need less information about the dataset.

The paper links this to the idea that SGD-like procedures can bias optimization toward wide valleys and thus implicitly reduce weight information.

\[ I(w;D)\le \tilde I(w;D)\le \frac12K\left[\log\|\hat w\|_2^2+\log\|H\|_*-K\log(K^2\beta/2)\right]. \]

The converse is not claimed: low information does not necessarily imply flatness. The paper explicitly notes this to avoid conflict with reparameterization critiques of flat-minima arguments Sec. 4.3.

Central dualitySec. 5, Props. 5.1-5.2

Result 7: Information in weights bounds information and total correlation in representations

This is the paper's most distinctive bridge: make weights less informative about the dataset, and under the stochastic multiplicative layer model the activations become less informative about the input and have lower total correlation. By Result 1, lower \(I(z;x)\) among sufficient representations means more invariance; lower \(TC(z)\) means more component independence.

For a single linear layer \(z=Wx\) with noisy weights and approximate Gaussian marginals:

\[ I(z;x)+TC(z)= -\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(z)}\mathbb{E}_{x}\log \frac{\tilde\alpha_i\hat W_i^2\cdot x^2} {\hat W_i\cdot Cov(x)\hat W_i+\tilde\alpha_i\hat W_i^2\cdot\mathbb{E}(x^2)}. \tag{6} \]

With additional assumptions, a uniform bound is

\[ g(\alpha)\le \frac{I(x;z)+TC(z)}{\dim(z)}\le g(\alpha)+c, \quad g(\alpha)=-\frac12\log(1-e^{-\alpha}), \quad c=O(1/\dim(x)). \]

The homogeneous-noise parameter is related to weight information by \(\alpha=\exp\{-I(W;D)/\dim(W)\}\) Prop. 5.2.

The layer's multiplicative weights turn uncertainty in \(W\) into uncertainty in \(z\). Under approximate Gaussianity, mutual information and total correlation reduce to log-variance ratios. The bounded-kurtosis argument controls a Taylor expansion and yields the clean uniform bound.

The identity is not a general theorem for arbitrary deterministic layers. It relies on stochastic weights, Gaussian approximations, and, for the simple bound, assumptions on the input components. The multi-layer bound in Corollary 5.3 may be loose; the paper says this is expected because a bottleneck bounds what can pass through, not what actually passes through.

PAC-BayesSec. 6, Eq. (7)

Result 8: The practical KL objective also arises from PAC-Bayes

For any \(\lambda>1/2\), prior \(p(w)\), and distribution \(q(w|D)\), the expected test error is bounded by a term with the same structure as Eq. (4): empirical cross-entropy plus a KL between posterior and prior.

\[ \mathbb{E}_D[L^{test}(q(w|D))] \le \frac{1}{N(1-\frac{1}{2\lambda})} \left(H_{p,q}(y|x,w)+\lambda L_{max}\mathbb{E}_D[KL(q(w|D)\|p(w))]\right). \tag{7} \]

The paper notes that the sharpest bound would use \(p(w)=q(w)\), the marginal over training randomness, making it equivalent to the weight IB. Since \(q(w)\) is intractable, the factorized approximation \(\tilde q(w)=\prod_i q(w_i)\) recovers Eq. (5).

Important equation walkthrough

The interactive panel follows the paper's main logic: representational compression, overfitting diagnosis, weight-information regularization, and activation-weight duality.

\[ L(p(z|x))=H(y|z)+\beta I(z;x) \]

Step 1: Classical activation information bottleneck

The first term pushes sufficiency: \(z\) should predict y. The second term pushes minimality: \(z\) should retain little about \(x\). The paper uses this as conceptual groundwork, then moves the bottleneck to weights Sec. 2.1, Eq. (1).

Step 2: Minimality gives invariance

Once \(z\) is sufficient, the excess \(I(z;x)-I(x;y)\) upper-bounds nuisance information. This is why minimizing \(I(z;x)\) is interpreted as learning invariance Sec. 3, Prop. 3.1.

Step 3: Cross-entropy can reward memorization

The negative \(-I(y;w|x,\theta)\) term says the loss can decrease when weights memorize label information not explained by the task distribution Sec. 4, Eq. (2).

Step 4: Weight bottleneck

Penalize \(I(w;D)\), because memorizing random labels requires information growing with \(N\), whereas learning the data-generating parameter can require bounded information in the model's idealization Sec. 4, Eq. (3).

Step 5: Practical stochastic-weight objective

Replace intractable \(I(w;D)\) by a KL to a prior or factorized marginal approximation. This also matches the PAC-Bayes form Secs. 4.1, 6.

Step 6: Duality

Under stochastic multiplicative weights, reducing weight information bounds \(I(z;x)+TC(z)\), hence pushes toward minimal and disentangled activations Sec. 5.

Method or algorithm

The paper is primarily theoretical, but it uses a concrete stochastic-weight training procedure to make \(I(w;D)\) controllable and testable.

Training recipe in the paper's model

  1. Choose a network and replace selected weights by stochastic weights \(w_i=\epsilon_i\hat w_i\).
  2. Use multiplicative log-normal noise for the theory, or Gaussian multiplicative noise in some experiments for stability App. B.
  3. Optimize cross-entropy plus \(\beta\) times a KL/information upper bound.
  4. Use the reparameterization trick/local reparameterization trick to train efficiently, following variational dropout methods Secs. 4.2, App. A.
  5. Control the effective complexity by sweeping \(\beta\): small \(\beta\) permits memorization; large \(\beta\) constrains information.

Pseudocode abstraction

\[ \begin{array}{ll} \textbf{Input:} & D,\; \beta,\; \text{network } f_w,\; q(w|D;\phi) \\ \textbf{Repeat:} & w\sim q(w|D;\phi) \\ & \widehat L=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{(x_i,y_i)} -\log q(y_i|x_i,w) \\ & \quad\quad +\;\beta\,KL(q(w|D;\phi)\|\tilde q(w)) \\ & \phi\leftarrow \phi-\eta\nabla_\phi \widehat L \\ \textbf{Return:} & q(w|D;\phi)\text{ or its mean/noisy predictor.} \end{array} \]

Difference from standard dropout: dropout injects noise but is not necessarily optimizing exactly this information bound. Variational dropout / information dropout style parameterizations make the information regularizer explicit and computable in the paper's setup.

Experiments

The experiments are best read as validation of the paper's modeling assumptions and qualitative predictions, not as exhaustive empirical proof of the theory for all architectures.

Experiment 1: Random-label phase transition

Question: Does controlling information in weights prevent memorization of random labels at the predicted threshold?

Setup: Small AlexNet on cropped CIFAR-10 with completely random labels; the paper also tests Small AlexNet, All-CNN, and ResNet on \(N=10000\) random labels. Training details include SGD with momentum 0.9, large batch size 500, and no weight decay or other regularization in the random-label experiment App. A.1.

Finding: For random labels, \(\beta<1\) allows overfitting; \(\beta>1\) leads to underfitting. Across architectures, Figure 2 shows a sharp transition near the critical value predicted by the theory Sec. 7.1, Figs. 1-2.

Experiment 2: Corrupted labels require more weight information

Question: Does fitting more random/corrupted labels require storing more information in the weights?

Setup: Fix \(\beta=0.1\), \(N=30000\), train to convergence over different label-corruption levels App. A.1.

Finding: As corruption increases, \(I(w;D)\) increases. For fully random 10-class labels, the increase is about \(3\) nats/sample, the same order as the \(\log 10\approx2.30\) nats needed to memorize a 10-class label Sec. 7.1, Fig. 2.

Experiment 3: Bias-variance trade-off via \(\beta\), not parameter count

Question: Does information in weights behave more like effective complexity than number of parameters?

Setup: All-CNN on CIFAR-10 with ZCA whitening, no data augmentation. One sweep changes number of filters/weights with weight decay; another keeps architecture fixed and changes \(\beta\) App. A.2.

Finding: Test error plateaus as parameter count grows, but sweeping \(\beta\) gives a characteristic underfit/fit/overfit curve. The paper interprets this as evidence that \(\tilde I(w;D)\) correlates better with generalization than raw parameter count Sec. 7.2, Fig. 3.

Experiment 4: Measuring nuisance information on cluttered MNIST

Question: Does reducing information in weights reduce \(I(z;n)\) for a controlled nuisance?

Setup: Generate cluttered MNIST by adding ten random \(4\times4\) occluding squares. Train classifiers with different \(\beta\), use last convolutional layer as \(z\), and train a discriminator to estimate \(\log p(z|n)/p(z)\) Sec. 7.3, App. A.3.

Finding: Lower weight information makes \(z\) increasingly insensitive to nuisance clutter while \(I(z;y)\) remains roughly constant. The paper cautions that the discriminator-based estimate has no formal quality guarantee Sec. 7.3, Fig. 4.

Supplementary visualization: CelebA reconstructions

Appendix A.4 trains a classifier for 40 CelebA binary attributes and a GAN-like reconstructor from representation \(z\). In Figure 5, increasing \(\beta\) makes reconstructed samples preserve task-relevant facial features such as hair color, sex, and expression while regenerating background, hairstyle, and other nuisance details. This visually supports the invariance interpretation, but it is a qualitative experiment rather than a theorem.

What the paper solves and does not solve

What is actually solved

  • A formal connection between sufficiency + information minimality and invariance to task nuisances Prop. 3.1.
  • An explanation for why bottlenecks, stacking, and noise can promote invariance when sufficiency is maintained Sec. 3.
  • A weight-information regularizer motivated by cross-entropy decomposition and by PAC-Bayes Secs. 4, 6.
  • Closed-form/computable upper bounds on weight information under multiplicative-noise assumptions Prop. 4.1.
  • A theoretical bridge from low weight information to low activation information and total correlation under a specific stochastic-layer model Sec. 5.
  • Empirical evidence for predicted random-label phase transitions and nuisance-insensitivity trends Sec. 7.

Limitations and open questions

  • Sufficiency is assumed in key places. If training fails or the test task differs, compression can remove useful information.
  • The stochastic representation view is essential. Deterministic networks are related via noise/flatness arguments, but the strongest results are not arbitrary deterministic-network guarantees.
  • Log-uniform priors simplify optimization but weaken PAC-Bayes numerics. The paper says the resulting test-error bound is vacuous under that improper prior.
  • The nuisance estimator is approximate. The discriminator method for \(I(z;n)\) has no guarantee on approximation quality Sec. 7.3.
  • Transfer learning is not addressed. The conclusion says using a representation optimized for one task on a different task is future work Sec. 8.
  • Data acquisition is orthogonal. By Data Processing, representation learning cannot create task information absent from the data; active learning and experiment design are outside the paper's scope Sec. 8.

Material intentionally skipped or de-emphasized

  • Most appendix proof algebra beyond the key proof ideas, because the paper's conceptual contribution is the chain of information inequalities and regularizers, not the line-by-line derivations.
  • Full architecture tables except where they matter for experimental interpretation. The paper's central empirical claims do not depend on memorizing every layer shape.
  • Reference-by-reference related work details, except where they distinguish this paper from activation-IB, PAC-Bayes, variational dropout, and flat-minima perspectives.
  • Implementation stabilizers for the GAN reconstruction experiment, because they do not alter the main theoretical claims.

Study guide

What to remember

  1. The paper's core representation theorem is: sufficient + minimal implies maximally invariant to nuisances.
  2. Minimality is about mutual information \(I(z;x)\), not dimensionality.
  3. The training-side innovation is the information bottleneck for weights: penalize \(I(w;D)\).
  4. For random labels, memorization needs information growing with \(N\), so \(\beta\approx1\) becomes a predicted transition point.
  5. Under stochastic multiplicative weights, low \(I(w;D)\) controls \(I(z;x)+TC(z)\), linking generalization, invariance, and disentanglement.

Common confusions

Is disentanglement the same as invariance?
No. Invariance is low information about nuisance variables \(I(z;n)\). Disentanglement is low dependence among components of \(z\), measured by total correlation \(TC(z)\). The paper links both to weight information, but they are different properties.
Does the paper say every SGD-trained network is invariant?
No. It argues SGD can implicitly reduce information in the weights under certain interpretations and cites related work. The rigorous representation conclusions still need sufficiency and the stated stochastic/Markov assumptions.
Why does \(\beta=1\) matter?
For random labels, reducing classification error requires storing label information in weights. The trade-off in Eq. (3) changes at \(\beta=1\): below it, memorization is favorable; above it, memorization is penalized more than it helps, in the idealized argument of Sec. 7.1.
Is parameter count irrelevant?
No. Parameter count can upper-bound capacity, but the paper argues it is too crude. The effective complexity relevant to these experiments is information stored in the learned weights.

Short quiz

1. In Proposition 3.1, why is sufficiency needed?
Without sufficiency, lowering \(I(z;x)\) could remove information about \(y\). Sufficiency fixes the amount of task information at \(I(x;y)\), so excess information can be interpreted as nuisance-related.
2. What does total correlation measure here?
\(TC(z)=KL(p(z)\|\prod_i p(z_i))\). It measures dependence among components. It is zero exactly when the components are independent.
3. Why is \(I(w;D)\) connected to overfitting random labels?
Random labels contain no structure in \(x\) or \(\theta\); fitting them means storing their identities in the weights. That storage cost grows roughly linearly with sample size.
4. Why is the PAC-Bayes connection useful but limited here?
It independently motivates the same KL-regularized objective. But the log-uniform prior used for the analytic information bound is improper, making the numerical PAC-Bayes generalization bound vacuous in the paper's model.
5. What assumption makes the multi-layer bound possible?
The layers must form a Markov chain so that Data Processing can be applied repeatedly. Skip connections complicate layer-level Markov structure, though the paper says ResNet blocks can still be considered.
