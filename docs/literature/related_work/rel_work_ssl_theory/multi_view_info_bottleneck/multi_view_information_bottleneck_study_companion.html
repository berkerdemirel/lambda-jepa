Learning Robust Representations via Multi-View Information Bottleneck

This paper asks how to get the robustness benefits of the supervised information bottleneck without labels. Its answer is: use two views of the same entity, treat information shared by both views as potentially task-relevant, and explicitly penalize view-private information.

Marco FedericiAnjan DuttaPatrick ForréNate KushmannZeynep Akata arXiv:2002.07017v2

1. Paper overview

One-paragraph summary. Classical information bottleneck learns a representation \(z\) that keeps label-relevant information while discarding nuisance information, but it needs labels \(y\) to know what is relevant. Federici et al. propose Multi-View Information Bottleneck (MIB): in an unsupervised two-view setting, if both views contain the same task-relevant information, then information unique to only one view is superfluous for that task. MIB therefore maximizes mutual information between the two learned view representations while penalizing a symmetrized KL divergence that aligns and compresses the stochastic encoders. The paper proves sufficiency statements under multi-view redundancy assumptions, derives the practical loss in Eq. (5), extends the idea to data augmentation in the single-view setting, and reports strong results on Sketchy, MIR-Flickr, and augmented MNIST. Sections 1–6; Eq. (5); Figures 1–4

Takeaway 1

The core trick is redundancy. If each view already contains all label information, then preserving cross-view information can preserve label information without observing labels. Definition 2; Corollary 1

Takeaway 2

MIB is not just InfoMax. MV-InfoMax keeps shared information but does not penalize view-private information; MIB adds an explicit compression/alignment term. Section 4; Eq. (5)

Takeaway 3

Augmentations become synthetic views. If transformations preserve labels, independently augmented versions of one input satisfy the redundancy story. Section 3.3; Theorem B.3

Takeaway 4

The assumption is doing real work. If mutual redundancy fails, aggressive compression can remove useful label information. The MIR-Flickr discussion and Appendix H make this explicit. Figure 6; Appendix H.2

2. Problem and motivation

The representation-learning goal

Learn an encoder \(p(z\mid x)\) that maps an observation \(x\) to a representation \(z\) useful for downstream tasks. In supervised information bottleneck, this means keeping information about \(y\) while minimizing information about \(x\) that is not needed for predicting \(y\). Section 2; Eqs. (1)–(2)

Supervised bottleneck intuition
\[\text{keep } I(y;z) \quad \text{but reduce } I(x;z\mid y).\]

The unsupervised obstacle

Without labels, reducing \(I(x;z)\) can destroy unknown task information. The paper formalizes this as a “no free generalization” statement: for a compressed representation, there exists some label for which the compression loses predictive information. Section 2; Theorem B.1

Why this matters: unsupervised compression needs an additional assumption about what information is safe to throw away.

The proposed additional assumption

Use paired observations \((v_1,v_2)\) of the same underlying entity. If both views are equally informative about the target, then view-specific information is treated as nuisance. This reframes “what is relevant?” as “what is shared across views?” Sections 1 and 3.1

3. Main contributions

1. Theory for unsupervised multi-view bottleneck

Claim: Under mutual redundancy, preserving all information shared by the two views is sufficient for label prediction, and private view information can be removed. Section 3.1; Corollary 1; Appendix B.3

Support: information-theoretic sufficiency and redundancy arguments.

2. Practical MIB objective

Claim: A tractable model can optimize a relaxed two-view objective using stochastic encoders, a sample-based MI lower bound, and symmetrized KL regularization. Section 3.2; Eq. (5); Appendix F

Support: derivation of an upper bound from the two Lagrangian losses.

3. Empirical evidence in multi- and single-view settings

Claim: MIB yields robust, data-efficient representations on Sketchy, MIR-Flickr, and augmented MNIST. Section 5; Tables 1–2; Figures 3–4

Support: retrieval/classification metrics and information-plane measurements.

4. Background and notation

The paper assumes standard information theory. The key is to read every mutual information term as answering: “what information does one variable reveal about another, possibly after conditioning?”

SymbolMeaning in this paperInterpretation
\(x\)Original observation in a single-view problem.An image, digit, or raw data point.
\(y\)Downstream label or target.May be unobserved during representation learning.
\(v_1, v_2\)Two paired views of the same entity.Image/sketch, image/tags, or two augmentations of one image.
\(z, z_1, z_2\)Learned latent representations.Outputs of stochastic encoders.
\(p_\theta(z_1\mid v_1), p_\psi(z_2\mid v_2)\)Parameterized stochastic encoders.Implemented as Gaussian distributions in the experiments. Appendix G.1
\(I(a;b)\)Mutual information.How much knowing \(a\) reduces uncertainty about \(b\).
\(I(a;b\mid c)\)Conditional mutual information.Residual dependence between \(a\) and \(b\) after observing \(c\).
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\)Kullback–Leibler divergence.How costly it is to treat samples from \(p\) as if they came from \(q\).
\(D_{\mathrm{SKL}}\)Symmetrized KL divergence.Average of both KL directions between the two stochastic encoders. Eq. (5); Appendix F
\(\beta\)Compression/robustness trade-off hyperparameter.\(\beta=0\) gives MV-InfoMax; too large can violate sufficiency. Section 4; Appendix H.2
Glossary: sufficiency, minimality, redundancy

Sufficiency: \(z\) is sufficient for \(y\) if \(I(x;y\mid z)=0\). Once \(z\) is known, \(x\) provides no extra information about \(y\). Definition 1

Minimality: among sufficient representations, discard as much input information as possible. In the supervised decomposition, this means reducing \(I(x;z\mid y)\). Eq. (2)

Redundancy: \(v_1\) is redundant with respect to \(v_2\) for \(y\) if \(I(y;v_1\mid v_2)=0\). Once \(v_2\) is known, \(v_1\) adds no label information. Definition 2

5. Mathematical setup and assumptions

Core setup

  1. Observe paired views \((v_1,v_2)\), not necessarily labels \(y\).
  2. Learn stochastic encoders \(p_\theta(z_1\mid v_1)\) and \(p_\psi(z_2\mid v_2)\).
  3. Make \(z_1\) sufficient for \(v_2\) and \(z_2\) sufficient for \(v_1\).
  4. Penalize view-private information via a KL alignment/compression term.

This is the abstract setup behind Figure 1 and Algorithm 1.

Most important assumption: mutual redundancy

Definition 2, symmetrized
\[I(y;v_1\mid v_2)=0 \quad \text{and}\quad I(y;v_2\mid v_1)=0.\]

Each view contains the same task-relevant information. The two views may contain different nuisances, but neither has label information unavailable in the other.

Assumption audit

Needed

Redundancy of views

Used to conclude that preserving cross-view information preserves label information. Without it, private information may include label signal. Corollary 1; Theorem B.2

Approximate in practice

Views share all label information

MIR-Flickr violates this approximately because tags are not always predictive of topic labels; the paper explicitly notes this. Figure 6; Section 5.1.2

Constructed

Label-preserving augmentation

For single-view data, transformations \(t\in\mathcal T\) must not affect label information: \(I(t(x);y)=I(x;y)\). Section 3.3; Proposition B.4

What would break the theory? If a view-specific feature is actually predictive of \(y\), MIB may treat it as nuisance and penalize it. This is why the choice of views or augmentations is not an implementation detail; it defines the invariances the method will learn.

6. Main theoretical results

These are the results worth retaining. Routine algebraic proofs are in the appendix; the central conceptual thread is below.

Result A: Sufficiency can be expressed as equality of predictive information

A representation \(z\) is sufficient for a label \(y\) precisely when it keeps all the information about \(y\) that was present in the input \(x\). Definition 1; Proposition B.1

Equation (1)
\[I(x;y\mid z)=0 \Longleftrightarrow I(x;y)=I(y;z).\]

Left side: \(x\) has no extra label information after seeing \(z\). Right side: the label information accessible from \(z\) equals that accessible from \(x\).

The proof uses the assumption that \(z\) is a representation of \(x\), so \(I(y;z\mid x)=0\), and rewrites \(I(x;y\mid z)\) using the multivariate mutual information chain rule. Appendix B.1

This is a definition-level statement, not an algorithm. It says how to recognize ideal sufficiency, but in unsupervised learning \(y\) is unavailable, so the paper needs the multi-view assumption.

Result B: Multi-view sufficiency implies label sufficiency under redundancy

If both views are mutually redundant for the label, and \(z_1\) keeps all information shared between \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), then \(z_1\) is as predictive for \(y\) as observing both views jointly. Corollary 1; Corollary B.2.1

Corollary 1
\[I(y;v_1\mid v_2)=I(y;v_2\mid v_1)=0,\quad I(v_1;v_2\mid z_1)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; I(y;z_1)=I(v_1v_2;y).\]

\(I(v_1;v_2\mid z_1)=0\) is sufficiency of \(z_1\) for \(v_2\): after seeing \(z_1\), the original \(v_1\) contains no additional information about \(v_2\).

The paper proves a more general lower bound:

Theorem B.2
\[I(y;z_1) \ge I(y;v_1v_2)-I(v_1;v_2\mid z_1)-I(v_1;y\mid v_2)-I(v_2;y\mid v_1).\]

Under exact redundancy, the last two penalty terms vanish. Under sufficiency for \(v_2\), the first penalty vanishes. Data processing gives the reverse inequality, so equality follows. Appendix B.3.1

The exact result requires exact redundancy and exact sufficiency, which learned neural models will only approximate. Theorem B.2 is useful because it shows how violations enter as explicit penalty terms. It does not say arbitrary views are safe.

Result C: Compression should target view-private information

The paper decomposes the information in \(z_1\) about \(v_1\) into information shared with \(v_2\) and information unique to \(v_1\). Under redundancy, the unique term is safe to remove. Section 3.1

Multi-view analogue of Eq. (2)
\[I(v_1;z_1)=I(v_1;z_1\mid v_2)+I(v_2;z_1).\]

\(I(v_2;z_1)\) is the information in \(z_1\) predictive of the other view; \(I(v_1;z_1\mid v_2)\) is view-private information.

This is the chain rule for mutual information, used with \(v_2\) playing the role of an observable proxy for the unknown label. The redundancy assumption turns “not shared with \(v_2\)” into “not needed for \(y\).”

This is not a claim that all shared information is label information. It may include common nuisances. The paper’s claim is that retaining shared information preserves label information under redundancy; minimality improves as views share less non-label content. Section 3.1; Appendix E.1

Result D: Label-preserving augmentations create valid synthetic views

If augmentations do not change label information, then two independent augmentations \(t_1(x)\) and \(t_2(x)\) can be treated as mutually redundant views. Section 3.3; Proposition B.4; Theorem B.3

Theorem B.3
\[I(t_1(x);y)=I(t_2(x);y)=I(x;y),\quad I(t_1(x);t_2(x)\mid z_1)=0 \Longrightarrow I(y;z_1)=I(x;y).\]

Proposition B.4 first shows label-preserving transformations imply mutual redundancy. Then the multi-view sufficiency result applies to the two augmented views.

The result explains why augmentation choice matters. If a transformation removes label information—e.g., cropping out an object or corrupting too aggressively—the assumptions fail. Appendix H.1 empirically shows MIB deteriorates when corruption approaches 100%.

7. Important equations and what they mean

Equation (2): supervised decomposition

\[I(x;z)=I(x;z\mid y)+I(y;z).\]

Bounded/controlled object: information retained about the input.

Meaning: if \(z\) is sufficient, \(I(y;z)\) is fixed at \(I(x;y)\), so minimizing \(I(x;z)\) becomes minimizing superfluous information \(I(x;z\mid y)\). Section 2

Equations (3)–(4): ideal two-view objectives

\[L_1(\theta;\lambda_1)=I_\theta(z_1;v_1\mid v_2)-\lambda_1 I_\theta(v_2;z_1),\]\[L_2(\psi;\lambda_2)=I_\psi(z_2;v_2\mid v_1)-\lambda_2 I_\psi(v_1;z_2).\]

Meaning: penalize private information while encouraging sufficiency for the opposite view. These are conceptually clean but not directly the final training loss. Section 3.2

Equation (5): MIB training loss

\[L_{\mathrm{MIB}}(\theta,\psi;\beta)=-I_{\theta\psi}(z_1;z_2)+\beta D_{\mathrm{SKL}}(p_\theta(z_1\mid v_1)\Vert p_\psi(z_2\mid v_2)).\]

Terms: maximize \(I(z_1;z_2)\) to make representations predict each other; minimize symmetrized KL to align and compress the stochastic encoders.

Regime: \(\beta=0\) recovers MV-InfoMax; increasing \(\beta\) increases compression, but \(\beta>1\) can prioritize minimality over sufficiency. Section 4; Appendix H.2

Appendix F: why KL appears

\[I_\theta(v_1;z_1\mid v_2)\le D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta(z_1\mid v_1)\Vert p_\psi(z_2\mid v_2)).\]

Meaning: the difficult private-information term is upper bounded by a KL between the two encoders’ distributions for paired views. Symmetrizing both directions yields \(D_{\mathrm{SKL}}\). Appendix F; Eq. (7)/(8)

8. Method and algorithm

MIB trains two stochastic encoders plus a mutual-information critic. The critic estimates a differentiable lower bound on \(I(z_1;z_2)\), while the encoder distributions allow direct KL evaluation. The paper tries Jensen–Shannon and InfoNCE estimators and reports main results with Jensen–Shannon because it performed better in these experiments. Section 3.2; Section 5; Appendix G.1; Table 2

Pseudocode-level summary of Algorithm 1
  1. If paired views exist, sample a minibatch \(\{(v_1^{(i)},v_2^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^B\).
  2. If using single-view data, sample \(x^{(i)}\) and two random augmentations \(t_1^{(i)},t_2^{(i)}\), then set \(v_1^{(i)}=t_1^{(i)}(x^{(i)})\), \(v_2^{(i)}=t_2^{(i)}(x^{(i)})\).
  3. Sample representations from the encoders: \(z_1^{(i)}\sim p_\theta(z_1\mid v_1^{(i)})\), \(z_2^{(i)}\sim p_\psi(z_2\mid v_2^{(i)})\).
  4. Compute the average symmetrized KL term on paired views.
  5. Subtract a sample-based MI estimate \(\hat I_\xi(\{(z_1^{(i)},z_2^{(i)})\})\).

This follows Figure 1 / Algorithm 1, with \(\beta\) weighting the KL term.

9. Experiments

The experiments test two related claims: MIB works for genuine multi-view tasks, and in a self-supervised setting it produces representations that retain label information while discarding more input information than InfoMax-style objectives.

9.1 Sketchy: sketch-based image retrieval

Question

Can MIB align sketch and image representations so an unseen query sketch retrieves images from the same class?

Protocol: Sketchy has 12,500 images and 75,471 sketches over 125 classes; the experiment adds 60,502 ImageNet images for 73,002 natural object images. It removes 6,250 sketches for testing, leaving 69,221 for training. Image/sketch VGG features are 4096-dimensional; encoders output 64-dimensional representations. Section 5.1.1; Appendix G.2

Interpretation

MIB performs best on mAP@all and is competitive on Prec@200. The paper attributes retrieval effectiveness partly to the symmetrized KL term, which not only removes view-private information but also aligns the two modalities. Table 1; Section 5.1.1

9.2 MIR-Flickr: image/tag multi-label classification

Question

Do image/tag representations learned without topic labels transfer to multi-label topic classification?

Protocol: images are represented by 3,857 handcrafted features \((v_1)\); the 2,000 most frequent user tags form a multi-hot tag view \((v_2)\). A logistic classifier is trained on learned image representations for 38 topics. Section 5.1.2; Appendix G.3

Interpretation

With all labels, MV-InfoMax and tuned MIB \((\beta=10^{-3})\) are similar. With fewer labels, stronger MIB compression \((\beta=1)\) helps, but it lowers full-label mAP. 10. What the paper solves and does not solve

What is solved

  • A principled way to adapt information bottleneck to unsupervised two-view representation learning.
  • A tractable loss with stochastic encoders and sample-based MI estimation. Eq. (5); Appendix F
  • A theoretical connection between data augmentation, invariance, and bottleneck learning. Section 3.3; Theorem B.3
  • Evidence that explicitly discarding view-private information can improve low-label performance. Figures 3–4; Table 2

What remains limited or open

  • Redundancy is strong. If views are not mutually redundant, compression can remove task information.
  • More than two views is non-trivial. Appendix D gives an XOR-style example showing mutual redundancy is not transitive.
  • Augmentation design is unresolved. The method learns invariances specified by augmentations; choosing them remains a modeling decision.
  • MI estimation is imperfect. Practical performance depends on neural MI estimators and critic architectures. Section 3.2; Appendix G.1
Special cases that clarify the objective

If the views share only label information: MIB is equivalent to supervised information bottleneck for the label, without observing the label. Appendix E.1

If the two views are identical: MIB degenerates to InfoMax because there is no view-private information to discard. Appendix E.2

If \(\beta=0\): the practical MIB objective becomes MV-InfoMax: it maximizes \(I(z_1;z_2)\) but has no explicit compression term. Section 4; Appendix H.2

Practical interpretation for deep learning researchers: MIB is most compelling when paired views or augmentations are known to preserve the downstream semantics while varying nuisance factors. It is not a general-purpose guarantee for arbitrary contrastive pairs.

12. Study guide

What to remember

  1. Supervised IB discards \(I(x;z\mid y)\), but needs \(y\).
  2. MIB replaces \(y\) with a redundant second view.
  3. The exact guarantee is: redundancy + view sufficiency \(\Rightarrow\) label sufficiency.
  4. The practical loss is MV-InfoMax plus a symmetrized KL compression/alignment term.
  5. Augmentation is a way to manufacture views, but only if it preserves labels.

Common confusions

Does MIB prove contrastive learning works?

No. It gives a bottleneck theory for a multi-view objective under redundancy assumptions. It is related to MV-InfoMax and contrastive objectives, but adds explicit compression and does not claim arbitrary contrastive learning is guaranteed.

Is all shared information label information?

No. Shared nuisances can remain. The result says label information is preserved under redundancy; minimality improves when views share fewer nuisances.

Why can \(\beta\) hurt?

Large \(\beta\) prioritizes compression. If redundancy is approximate or the estimator/optimization is imperfect, compression can remove useful information. Appendix H.2

Short quiz

1. What is the difference between InfoMax, MV-InfoMax, and MIB?

InfoMax maximizes information between input and representation. MV-InfoMax maximizes information between representations/views. MIB adds a compression/alignment penalty so the representation keeps shared information while discarding view-private information. Section 4; Eq. (5)

2. Why is Theorem B.1 important?

It says compression alone cannot be universally safe: for any compressed representation, there exists some label task where predictive information is lost. This motivates the need for view redundancy or augmentation assumptions.

3. What does \(I(v_1;z_1\mid v_2)\) represent?

The information in the representation of view 1 that is not predictable from view 2: view-private information. Under mutual redundancy, this is treated as superfluous for the target.

4. What empirical result best supports the bottleneck interpretation?

The MNIST information-plane experiment: MIB \((\beta=1,I_{JS})\) reports low \(I(x;z)\) and high \(I(z;y)\), with much higher low-label accuracy than InfoMax and MV-InfoMax. Figure 4; Table 2

5. What is the biggest modeling risk?

Choosing views or augmentations that do not preserve all label information. In that case, what MIB treats as view-private nuisance may be task-relevant.
